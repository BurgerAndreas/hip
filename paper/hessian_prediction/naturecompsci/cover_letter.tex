\noindent
\textbf{RE: INVITED MANUSCRIPT SUBMISSION \textendash\ ``Shoot from the HIP: Hessian Interatomic Potentials without Derivatives''}

\vspace{13pt}

\noindent Dear Dr.~McCardle,

On behalf of my co-authors, I am pleased to submit our invited manuscript, ``Shoot from the HIP: Hessian Interatomic Potentials without Derivatives'' for consideration at Nature Computational Science. This submission follows your invitation after the work received one of the \textbf{Best Paper Awards at the EurIPS Symbiochem workshop}, and we are grateful for the opportunity to submit an expanded and strengthened version for your journal's broad computational-science readership.

Accurate second-order information is central to modern computational chemistry and materials science: molecular Hessians underpin vibrational analysis, transition-state searches, robust second-order geometry optimization, and much more. The steep scaling of Hessians compared to energies and forces often renders them the limiting factor for applying computational methods at scale and in high-throughput screenings. While modern machine-learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) have made energies and forces inexpensive to compute, \textbf{Hessians remain challenging} in practice: obtaining second derivatives through automatic differentiation (AD) or finite-difference approaches is costly, scaling as $O(N_{\mathrm{atoms}}^{3})$ in terms of MLIP evaluations, and memory-intensive. Additionally, MLIPs often fail to faithfully reproduce DFT Hessians, particularly due to the absence of dedicated, expensive Hessian training. This gap leaves many workflows without reliable, scalable access to curvature information even when accurate MLIPs are available.

In this work, we introduce \textbf{Hessian Interatomic Potentials (HIP)}, a learning framework that \textbf{directly predicts molecular Hessians} using an equivariant graph neural network (GNN) while respecting required physical structure (e.g., symmetry and equivariance constraints), enabling fast and stable second-order information without resorting to finite differences or differentiating the model twice. This reduces the computational cost from $O(N_{\mathrm{atoms}}^{3})$ to $O(1)$ in terms of GNN forward passes, making the approach much more scalable. In our benchmarks, this yields almost \textbf{two orders of magnitude in speedup} alongside improved Hessian fidelity and consistently better downstream outcomes (e.g., transition-state search success, geometry optimization, and more accurate ZPEs) compared to MLIPs trained on the same data. We are confident that the sharply reduced

{\looseness=-2
We have also directly addressed your comments to validate HIP in a size-extrapolative setting and compare its performance to MLIP Hessians obtained with AD. For this, we tested HIP on the RGD1 dataset with molecules of 30 atoms, about 50\% larger than those in our training dataset, as well as on a small subset of PubChem molecules with 100 atoms, about five times the number of atoms in the training dataset. We show that HIP's performance in terms of the eigenvalues and mean square error stays largely constant, whereas the MLIP AD-Hessian suffer significantly more. We believe these additions strengthen the manuscript's relevance to the journal's broad computational science readership by demonstrating not only methodological novelty but also clear operational advantages on challenging, practically relevant systems.
}
